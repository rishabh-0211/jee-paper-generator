### Q1. JEE Main 2026 (28 January Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

A nucleus has mass number $\alpha$ and radius $R_{\alpha}$. Another nucleus has mass number $\beta$ and radius $R_{\beta}$. If $\beta=8 \alpha$ then $R_{\alpha} / R_{\beta}$ is:

**Options:**

- **(A)** 8
- **(B)** 0.5
- **(C)** 2
- **(D)** 1

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) 0.5**

**Detailed Solution:**

Nuclear radius follows $R = R_0 A^{1/3}$ where $A$ is mass number. 
Given $\beta = 8\alpha$, we have $R_\alpha = R_0 \alpha^{1/3}$ and $R_\beta = R_0 (8\alpha)^{1/3} = R_0 \cdot 2 \alpha^{1/3} = 2R_\alpha$. 
Thus $R_\alpha/R_\beta = 1/2 = 0.5$.

</details>

### Q3. JEE Main 2026 (23 January Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

Which of the following pair of nuclei are isobars of the element?

**Options:**

- **(A)** ${ }_{1}^{3} \mathrm{H}$ and ${ }_{2}^{3} \mathrm{He}$
- **(B)** ${ }_{1}^{2} \mathrm{H}$ and ${ }_{1}^{3} \mathrm{H}$
- **(C)** ${ }_{80}^{198} \mathrm{Hg}$ and ${ }_{79}^{197} \mathrm{Au}$
- **(D)** ${ }_{92}^{236} \mathrm{U}$ and ${ }_{92}^{238} \mathrm{U}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) ${ }_{1}^{3} \mathrm{H}$ and ${ }_{2}^{3} \mathrm{He}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Isobars are atoms of different chemical elements that have the same number of nucleons (mass number $A$), but different atomic numbers ($Z$).
In the given options:
(1) $_1^3\text{H}$ and $_2^3\text{He}$: Both have the same mass number $A = 3$. Since they have different atomic numbers ($Z=1$ and $Z=2$), they are isobars.
(2) $_1^2\text{H}$ and $_1^3\text{H}$: These have the same atomic number ($Z=1$) but different mass numbers. They are isotopes.
(3) $_{80}^{198}\text{Hg}$ and $_{79}^{197}\text{Au}$: These have different mass numbers ($198$ and $197$).
(4) $_{92}^{236}\text{U}$ and $_{92}^{238}\text{U}$: These have the same atomic number ($Z=92$) but different mass numbers. They are isotopes.
Thus, the pair $_1^3\text{H}$ and $_2^3\text{He}$ are isobars.

</details>

### Q4. JEE Main 2026 (22 January Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

$7.9 \mathrm{MeV} \alpha$-particle scatters from a target material of atomic number 79. From the given data the estimated diameter of nuclei of the target material is (approximately) $\_\_\_\_$ m. $\left[\frac{1}{4 \pi \epsilon_{\mathrm{o}}}=9 \times 10^{9} \mathrm{Nm}^{2} / \mathrm{C}^{2}\right.$ and electron charge $\left.=1.6 \times 10^{-19} \mathrm{C}\right]$

**Options:**

- **(A)** $1.44 \times 10^{-13}$
- **(B)** $5.76 \times 10^{-14}$
- **(C)** $2.88 \times 10^{-14}$
- **(D)** $1.69 \times 10^{-12}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $5.76 \times 10^{-14}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

For Rutherford scattering, at the closest approach all kinetic energy converts to electrostatic potential energy.
For alpha particle-nucleus collision:
$K.E. = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{r_{min}}$
Where: $Z_1 = 2$ (alpha), $Z_2 = 79$ (gold), $K.E. = 7.9$ MeV
$r_{min} = \frac{9 \times 10^9 \times 2 \times 79 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{7.9 \times 10^6 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}}$
$r_{min} = 2.88 \times 10^{-14}$ m
The estimated nuclear diameter is approximately:
$d = 2 \times r_{min} = 5.76 \times 10^{-14}$ m

</details>

### Q5. JEE Main 2025 (8 Apr Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

For a nucleus of mass number $A$ and radius $R$, the mass density of nucleus can be represented as

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\mathrm{A}^3$
- **(B)** $\mathrm{A}^{\frac{1}{3}}$
- **(C)** $\mathrm{A}^{\frac{2}{3}}$
- **(D)** Independent of A

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(D) Independent of A**

**Detailed Solution:**

The mass density ( $\rho$) of a nucleus is defined as the ratio of its mass to its volume:
$\rho=$ Mass of nucleus / Volume of nucleus
For a spherical nucleus with radius R , the volume is:
$\text { Volume }=(4 / 3) \pi R^3$
The mass of the nucleus is related to the mass number A :
$\text { Mass }-\mathrm{A} \times \mathrm{m} \text { _u }$
(where m_u is the atomic mass unit, approximately $1.66 \times 10^{-27} \mathrm{~kg}$)
Therefore:
$\rho=\left(\mathrm{A} \times \mathrm{m} \_\mathrm{u}\right) /\left[(4 / 3) \pi \mathrm{R}^3\right]$
Now, there's an important relationship between the mass number A and nuclear radius R. Empirically, it has been found that:
$\mathrm{R}=\mathrm{Ro} * \mathrm{~A}^{\wedge}(1 / 3)$
Where Ro is a constant approximately equal to $1.2 \times 10^{-15} \mathrm{~m}(1.2$ fermi).
Substituting this into the density equation:
$\begin{aligned}
& \rho=\left(\mathrm{A} \times \mathrm{m} \_\mathrm{u}\right) /\left[(4 / 3) \pi\left(\mathrm{Ro} \times \mathrm{A}^{\wedge}(1 / 3)\right)^3\right] \\
& \rho=(\mathrm{A} \times \mathrm{m} \text { _u }) /\left[(4 / 3) \pi \mathrm{Ro}^3 \times \mathrm{A}\right] \\
& \rho=\mathrm{m} \_\mathrm{u} /\left[(4 / 3) \pi \mathrm{Ro}^3\right]
\end{aligned}$
This shows that the nuclear density is approximately constant for all nuclei, regardless of their mass number. This is a fundamental property of nuclear matter known as nuclear saturation density.
The numerical value is:
$\rho=2.3 \times 10^{17} \mathrm{~kg} / \mathrm{m}^3$

</details>
