### Q1. MHT CET 2026 (14 May Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

The magnitude of gravitational field at distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ from the centre of a uniform sphere of radius R and mass M are $I_1$ and $I_2$ respectively. Given that $r_1 > R \text{ and } r_2 < R$, the ratio $\dfrac{I_1}{I_2}$ is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\dfrac{R^2}{r_1 r_2}$
- **(B)** $\dfrac{R^3}{r_1 r_2^2}$
- **(C)** $\dfrac{R^3}{r_1^2 r_2}$
- **(D)** $\dfrac{R^4}{r_1^2 r_2^2}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $\dfrac{R^3}{r_1^2 r_2}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

The magnitude of gravitational field at a distance $r$ from the centre of a uniform solid sphere of mass $M$ and radius $R$ is given by:

For a point outside the sphere ($r > R$), $I = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}$

For a point inside the sphere ($r < R$), $I = \dfrac{GMr}{R^3}$

Given $r_1 > R$, the gravitational field is $I_1 = \dfrac{GM}{r_1^2}$

Given $r_2 < R$, the gravitational field is $I_2 = \dfrac{GMr_2}{R^3}$

Taking the ratio of the two fields:

$\dfrac{I_1}{I_2} = \dfrac{\dfrac{GM}{r_1^2}}{\dfrac{GMr_2}{R^3}}$

$\dfrac{I_1}{I_2} = \dfrac{GM}{r_1^2} \times \dfrac{R^3}{GMr_2}$

$\dfrac{I_1}{I_2} = \dfrac{R^3}{r_1^2 r_2}$

</details>

### Q2. MHT CET 2026 (14 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

Two particles of masses $m$ and $M$ are placed a distance $d$ apart. The gravitational potential at the position where the gravitational field due to them is zero is $V$. Then

**Options:**

- **(A)** $V = -\dfrac{G}{d}(m + M)$
- **(B)** $V = -\dfrac{Gm}{d}$
- **(C)** $V = -\dfrac{GM}{d}$
- **(D)** $V = -\dfrac{G}{d}(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})^2$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(D) $V = -\dfrac{G}{d}(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})^2$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Let the point where the gravitational field is zero be at a distance $x$ from the mass $m$. The distance from the mass $M$ will be $d - x$.

Equating the magnitudes of the gravitational fields due to both masses at this point:

$\dfrac{Gm}{x^2} = \dfrac{GM}{(d-x)^2}$

Taking the square root of both sides:

$\dfrac{\sqrt{m}}{x} = \dfrac{\sqrt{M}}{d-x}$

Solving for $x$:

$x\sqrt{M} = (d-x)\sqrt{m}$

$x(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M}) = d\sqrt{m}$

$x = \dfrac{d\sqrt{m}}{\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M}}$

The distance from the mass $M$ is:

$d - x = \dfrac{d\sqrt{M}}{\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M}}$

The gravitational potential $V$ at this point is the sum of the potentials due to both masses:

$V = -\dfrac{Gm}{x} - \dfrac{GM}{d-x}$

Substituting the values of $x$ and $d-x$:

$V = -\dfrac{Gm(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})}{d\sqrt{m}} - \dfrac{GM(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})}{d\sqrt{M}}$

$V = -\dfrac{G\sqrt{m}(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})}{d} - \dfrac{G\sqrt{M}(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})}{d}$

$V = -\dfrac{G}{d}(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})$

$V = -\dfrac{G}{d}(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})^2$

Answer: $V = -\dfrac{G}{d}(\sqrt{m} + \sqrt{M})^2$

</details>

### Q4. MHT CET 2026 (11 April Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

The density of a planet is three times that of earth and radius $2.5$ times that of earth. If $g_p$ and $g_e$ represents acceleration due to gravity on planet and earth respectively then ratio $g_p$ to $g_e$ is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $7.5$
- **(B)** $3.5$
- **(C)** $6.5$
- **(D)** $9.5$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $7.5$**

**Detailed Solution:**

The acceleration due to gravity on the surface of a planet is given by

$g = \dfrac{GM}{R^2}$

Substituting the mass of the planet $M = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho$, we get

$g = \dfrac{G}{R^2} \left(\dfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho\right) = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi G R \rho$

This implies that $g \propto R \rho$.

The ratio of acceleration due to gravity on the planet to that on Earth is

$\dfrac{g_p}{g_e} = \dfrac{R_p \rho_p}{R_e \rho_e}$

Given that $\rho_p = 3\rho_e$ and $R_p = 2.5R_e$, substituting these values yields

$\dfrac{g_p}{g_e} = (2.5) \times (3) = 7.5$

Answer: $7.5$

</details>

### Q6. MHT CET 2025 (23 Apr Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

A uniform solid sphere of mass ' m ' and radius ' r ' is surrounded by a uniform thin spherical shell of radius ' $2 r$ ' and mass ' $m$ ' then the gravitational field

**Options:**

- **(A)** at a distance of 15 r from the centre is $\frac{2}{9} \frac{\mathrm{Gm}}{\mathrm{r}^2}$
- **(B)** at a distance of $(2.5) \mathrm{r}$ from the centre is $\frac{8}{25} \frac{\mathrm{Gm}}{\mathrm{r}^2}$
- **(C)** at a distance of $(1.5) \mathrm{r}$ from the centre is zero.
- **(D)** between the sphere and spherical shell is uniform.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) at a distance of $(2.5) \mathrm{r}$ from the centre is $\frac{8}{25} \frac{\mathrm{Gm}}{\mathrm{r}^2}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

For a point outside both the solid sphere and the spherical shell ($x > 2r$), both objects behave as point masses concentrated at their common centre. The total effective mass is $M = m + m = 2m$.

At a distance $x = 2.5r$, the gravitational field is:

$E = \dfrac{G(2m)}{(2.5r)^2}$

$E = \dfrac{2Gm}{6.25r^2}$

$E = \dfrac{2Gm}{\left(\dfrac{25}{4}\right)r^2}$

$E = \dfrac{8}{25}\dfrac{Gm}{r^2}$

For a point between the sphere and the shell ($r < x < 2r$), the gravitational field due to the outer shell is zero. The field is solely due to the inner solid sphere, given by $E = \dfrac{Gm}{x^2}$. This field is neither zero at $x = 1.5r$ nor uniform.

Answer: at a distance of $(2.5) \mathrm{r}$ from the centre is $\frac{8}{25} \frac{\mathrm{Gm}}{\mathrm{r}^2}$

</details>

### Q7. MHT CET 2024 (04 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

If ' $R$ ' is the radius of earth \& ' $g$ ' is acceleration due to gravity on earth's surface, then mean density of earth is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\dfrac{4 \pi \mathrm{G}}{3 g R}$
- **(B)** $\dfrac{3 \pi R}{4 g G}$
- **(C)** $\dfrac{3 \mathrm{~g}}{4 \pi \mathrm{RG}}$
- **(D)** $\dfrac{\pi \mathrm{RG}}{12 \mathrm{~g}}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $\dfrac{3 \mathrm{~g}}{4 \pi \mathrm{RG}}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

The acceleration due to gravity on the surface of the earth is given by

$g = \dfrac{GM}{R^2}$

The mass of the earth $M$ can be written in terms of its mean density $\rho$ and volume $V$ as

$M = \dfrac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \rho$

Substituting the value of $M$ in the expression for $g$

$g = \dfrac{G}{R^2} \left( \dfrac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \rho \right)$

$g = \dfrac{4}{3} \pi R G \rho$

Rearranging the above equation for density $\rho$

$\rho = \dfrac{3g}{4 \pi R G}$

Answer: $\dfrac{3 \mathrm{~g}}{4 \pi \mathrm{RG}}$

</details>

### Q11. MHT CET 2011

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If the density of a small planet is the same as that of earth, while the radius of the planet is
$0.2$ times that the earth, the gravitational acceleration on the surface of that planet is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $0.2 \mathrm{~g}$
- **(B)** $0.4 \mathrm{~g}$
- **(C)** $2 \mathrm{~g}$
- **(D)** $4 \bar{g}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $0.2 \mathrm{~g}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

$g=\frac{4}{3} \pi G R \rho$
and
$$
g^{\prime}=\frac{4}{3} \pi G R^{\prime} \rho
$$
$\therefore$
$$
\begin{array}{l}
\frac{g^{\prime}}{g}=\frac{R^{\prime}}{R}=0.2 \\
g^{\prime}=0.2 g
\end{array}
$$

### Q12. MHT CET 2010

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `3`

If the density of the earth is doubled keeping radius constant, find the new acceleration due to gravity? $\left(g=9.8 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}\right)$

**Options:**

- **(A)** $9.8 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$
- **(B)** $19.6 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$
- **(C)** $4.9 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$
- **(D)** $39.2 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $19.6 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Acceleration due to gravity $g=\frac{4}{3} \pi \rho G R$
or $g \propto d R$
$$
\left[\because \text { density } d=\frac{4}{3} \pi \rho\right]
$$
$$
\begin{aligned}
\therefore \quad & \frac{g_{1}}{g_{2}}=\frac{d_{1}}{d_{2}} \\
\frac{g_{1}}{g_{2}} &=\frac{d}{2 d} \quad\left(\because d_{2}=2 d\right) \\
& g_{2}=g_{1} \times 2=9.8 \times 2 \\
& g_{2}=19.6 \mathrm{~m} / \mathrm{s}^{2}
\end{aligned}
$$

</details>
