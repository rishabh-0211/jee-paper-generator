### Q1. MHT CET 2026 (12 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `3`

Four particles, each of mass M and equidistant from each other, move along a circle of radius R under the action of their gravitational attraction, the speed of each particle is $\{\sin(45)^0=\cos(45)^\circ=1/(2)^{0.5}\}$

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}}$
- **(B)** $\sqrt{2\sqrt{2}\dfrac{GM}{R}}$
- **(C)** $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}(1+2\sqrt{2})}$
- **(D)** $v=\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}(1+2\sqrt{2})}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(D) $v=\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}(1+2\sqrt{2})}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

The four particles are located at the vertices of a square inscribed in a circle of radius $R$.

The diagonal of the square is $2R$, so the side length of the square is $a = \sqrt{2}R$.

Let us consider the net gravitational force acting on one of the particles. The force is due to the other three particles: one at the opposite vertex and two at the adjacent vertices.

The force due to the particle at the opposite vertex is directed towards the center:
$F_1 = \dfrac{GM^2}{(2R)^2} = \dfrac{GM^2}{4R^2}$

The force due to each of the two adjacent particles is:
$F_2 = \dfrac{GM^2}{a^2} = \dfrac{GM^2}{(\sqrt{2}R)^2} = \dfrac{GM^2}{2R^2}$

The angle between the force $F_2$ and the diagonal is $45^\circ$. The components of these two forces directed towards the center add up, while the perpendicular components cancel out.

The net force towards the center is:
$F_{net} = F_1 + 2F_2 \cos 45^\circ$

$F_{net} = \dfrac{GM^2}{4R^2} + 2 \left( \dfrac{GM^2}{2R^2} \right) \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$

$F_{net} = \dfrac{GM^2}{R^2} \left( \dfrac{1}{4} + \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \right) = \dfrac{GM^2}{4R^2} (1 + 2\sqrt{2})$

This net gravitational force provides the necessary centripetal force for the particle to move in a circular path of radius $R$:
$\dfrac{Mv^2}{R} = \dfrac{GM^2}{4R^2} (1 + 2\sqrt{2})$

$v^2 = \dfrac{GM}{4R} (1 + 2\sqrt{2})$

$v = \dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}(1+2\sqrt{2})}$

Answer: $v=\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}(1+2\sqrt{2})}$

</details>

### Q2. MHT CET 2025 (23 Apr Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

The gravitational pull of the moon is $\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{\text {th }}$ of the earth and mass of moon is $\left(\frac{1}{8}\right)^{\text {th }}$ of the earth. This implies that the

**Options:**

- **(A)** radius of moon is $(1 / 4)^{\text {th }}$ of the earth's radius.
- **(B)** radius of the earth is $(\sqrt{4 / 3})^{\text {th }}$ of the moon's radius.
- **(C)** moon's radius is half that of the earth.
- **(D)** radius of the earth is $(4 / 3)^{\text {th }}$ of the moon's radius.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) radius of the earth is $(\sqrt{4 / 3})^{\text {th }}$ of the moon's radius.**

**Detailed Solution:**

The acceleration due to gravity on the surface of a planet is given by $g = \dfrac{GM}{R^2}$.

Rearranging for radius squared, we get $R^2 = \dfrac{GM}{g}$.

Taking the ratio of the square of the radius of the earth to that of the moon:

$\dfrac{R_e^2}{R_m^2} = \left(\dfrac{M_e}{M_m}\right) \times \left(\dfrac{g_m}{g_e}\right)$

Given that $M_m = \dfrac{1}{8} M_e \Rightarrow \dfrac{M_e}{M_m} = 8$

And $g_m = \dfrac{1}{6} g_e \Rightarrow \dfrac{g_m}{g_e} = \dfrac{1}{6}$

Substituting these values into the ratio:

$\dfrac{R_e^2}{R_m^2} = 8 \times \dfrac{1}{6} = \dfrac{4}{3}$

Taking the square root on both sides:

$\dfrac{R_e}{R_m} = \sqrt{\dfrac{4}{3}}$

$R_e = \sqrt{\dfrac{4}{3}} R_m$

Thus, the radius of the earth is $\left(\sqrt{\dfrac{4}{3}}\right)^{\text{th}}$ of the moon's radius.

Answer: radius of the earth is $(\sqrt{4 / 3})^{\text {th }}$ of the moon's radius.

</details>

### Q3. MHT CET 2024 (11 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

Assuming that the earth is revolving around the sun in circular orbit of radius $R$, the angular momentum is directly proportional to $\mathrm{R}^{\mathrm{n}}$. The value of ' $n$ ' is

**Options:**

- **(A)** 2.
- **(B)** 1.5
- **(C)** 1
- **(D)** 0.5

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(D) 0.5**

**Detailed Solution:**

The orbital velocity of the earth revolving around the sun is given by $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}}$.

The angular momentum of the earth is $L = m v R$.

Substituting the value of $v$, we get $L = m \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R}} R = m \sqrt{GM} R^{1/2}$.

Since $m$, $G$, and $M$ are constants, $L \propto R^{1/2}$.

Comparing this with $L \propto R^n$, we get $n = 0.5$.

Answer: 0.5

</details>

### Q4. MHT CET 2024 (03 May Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `3`

Two identical metal spheres are kept in contact with each other, each having radius ' $R$ ' cm and ' $\rho$ ' is the density of material of metal spheres. The gravitational force ' $F$ ' of attraction between them is proportional to

**Options:**

- **(A)** $R^3 \rho$
- **(B)** $R^4 p^2$
- **(C)** $R^4 \rho$
- **(D)** $R^3 \rho^2$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $R^4 p^2$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Mass of each metal sphere is given by $M = \text{Volume} \times \text{Density} = \dfrac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \rho$

Since the two identical spheres are kept in contact, the distance between their centers is $d = R + R = 2R$.

The gravitational force of attraction between them is:

$F = \dfrac{G M_1 M_2}{d^2} = \dfrac{G M^2}{(2R)^2}$

Substituting the value of $M$:

$F = \dfrac{G \left(\dfrac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \rho\right)^2}{4R^2}$

$F = \dfrac{G \times \dfrac{16}{9} \pi^2 R^6 \rho^2}{4R^2}$

$F = \dfrac{4}{9} G \pi^2 R^4 \rho^2$

Therefore, $F \propto R^4 \rho^2$.

Answer: $R^4 p^2$

</details>

### Q7. MHT CET 2020 (15 Oct Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

The acceleration due to gravity on moon is $\left(\frac{1}{6}\right)^{\text {th }}$ times the acceleration due to gravity on earth. If the ratio of the density of earth ' $\varrho_{e}$ ' to the density of moon $\varrho_{m}$ ' is $\frac{5}{3}$, then the radius of moon ' $\mathrm{Rm}$ ' in terms of the radius of earth 'Re' is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\left(\frac{7}{6}\right) \operatorname{Re}$
- **(B)** $\left(\frac{3}{18}\right) \mathrm{Re}$
- **(C)** $\left(\frac{5}{18}\right) \mathrm{Re}$
- **(D)** $\left(\frac{1}{2 \sqrt{3}}\right) \mathrm{Re}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $\left(\frac{5}{18}\right) \mathrm{Re}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

$\frac{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}}=\frac{1}{6} \quad \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}=\frac{5}{3} \quad \frac{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{e}}}=?$
$\frac{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{g}_{\mathrm{e}}}=\frac{\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}} \times \frac{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}}{\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{e}}}=\frac{\frac{4}{3} \pi \mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{m}}^{3} \rho_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{m}}^{2}} \times \frac{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{e}}^{2}}{\frac{4}{3} \pi \mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{e}}^{3} \rho_{\mathrm{e}}}=\frac{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{m}} \rho_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{e}} \rho_{\mathrm{e}}}=\frac{1}{6}$
$\therefore \frac{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{m}}}{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{e}}}=\frac{1}{6} \times \frac{\rho_{\mathrm{e}}}{\rho_{\mathrm{m}}}=\frac{1}{6} \times \frac{5}{3}=\frac{5}{18}$

### Q8. MHT CET 2020 (14 Oct Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

A body is projected vertically upwards from earth's surface. If its K.E. of projection is
equal to half of its minimum value required to escape from the gravitational
influence, then the height upto which it rises is $(\mathrm{R}=$ radius of the earth $)$

**Options:**

- **(A)** $4 R$
- **(B)** $\mathrm{R}$
- **(C)** $2 \mathrm{R}$
- **(D)** $3 \mathrm{R}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $\mathrm{R}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Minimum value of kinetic energy required to escape from the gravitational influence of the earth is given by
$\mathrm{k}_{\mathrm{e}}=\frac{\mathrm{GMm}}{\mathrm{R}}$
$\therefore$ The kinetic energy of projection is
$\mathrm{K}=\frac{\mathrm{GMm}}{2 \mathrm{R}}$
At the highest point the kinetic energy become zero.
Loss of kinetic energy $=$ Gain in potential energy
$\begin{aligned}
& \frac{\mathrm{GMm}}{2 \mathrm{R}}=\frac{\mathrm{GMm}}{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{\mathrm{GMm}}{\mathrm{R}+\mathrm{h}} \\
\therefore & \frac{1}{2 \mathrm{R}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{1}{\mathrm{R}+\mathrm{h}} \\
\therefore & \frac{1}{\mathrm{R}+\mathrm{h}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{R}}-\frac{1}{2 \mathrm{R}}=\frac{1}{2 \mathrm{R}} \\
\therefore & \mathrm{R}+\mathrm{h}=2 \mathrm{R} \\
\therefore & \mathrm{h}=2 \mathrm{R}-\mathrm{R}=\mathrm{R}
\end{aligned}$

</details>
