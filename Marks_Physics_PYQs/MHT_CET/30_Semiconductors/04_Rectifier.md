### Q1. MHT CET 2025 (22 Apr Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If a centre tap transformer is used with two p-n junction diodes for full wave rectification then output voltage of rectifier with respect to each diode is (secondary voltage of transformer $=V_s$ )

**Options:**

- **(A)** $2 \mathrm{~V}_{\mathrm{s}}$
- **(B)** $\left(\frac{2}{3}\right) V_s$
- **(C)** $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) V_s$
- **(D)** $\left(\frac{3}{2}\right) V_s$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) V_s$**

**Detailed Solution:**

In a center-tap transformer used for full-wave rectification, the secondary winding is divided into two equal halves by the center tap.

If the total voltage across the secondary winding of the transformer is $V_s$, the voltage across each half of the secondary winding is $\dfrac{V_s}{2}$.

During each half cycle of the AC input, only one half of the secondary winding provides power to the load through its corresponding forward-biased diode. 

Therefore, the output voltage of the rectifier, which is the voltage across the load, is equal to the voltage across half of the secondary winding, which is $\dfrac{1}{2} V_s$.

Answer: $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) V_s$

</details>

### Q2. MHT CET 2024 (15 May Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

A p-n junction diode as a rectifier converts

**Options:**

- **(A)** a.c. to d.c.
- **(B)** d.c. to a.c.
- **(C)** high voltage to low voltage.
- **(D)** low voltage to high voltage.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) a.c. to d.c.**

**Detailed Solution:**

A p-n junction diode allows electric current to flow in only one direction when it is forward biased and blocks the current when it is reverse biased.

Because of this unidirectional property, it is used as a rectifier to convert alternating current (a.c.) into direct current (d.c.).

Answer: a.c. to d.c.

</details>

### Q3. MHT CET 2024 (10 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

In the block diagram of simple rectifier circuit, from a variable a.c. voltage, constant d.c. voltage is obtained. The correct order of operation is

**Options:**

- **(A)** voltage regulator, diode rectifier, filter.
- **(B)** diode rectifier, voltage regulator, filter.
- **(C)** diode rectifier, filter, voltage regulator.
- **(D)** filter, voltage regulator, diode rectifier.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) diode rectifier, filter, voltage regulator.**

**Detailed Solution:**

The alternating current (a.c.) voltage is first applied to a diode rectifier, which converts the a.c. into pulsating direct current (d.c.).

The pulsating d.c. output is then passed through a filter circuit to remove the a.c. ripples and provide a smoother d.c. voltage.

Finally, the output from the filter is fed to a voltage regulator to obtain a constant, steady d.c. voltage that is independent of load variations.

Therefore, the correct order of operation is diode rectifier, filter, voltage regulator.

Answer: diode rectifier, filter, voltage regulator.

</details>

### Q7. MHT CET 2021 (24 Sep Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

**Options:**

- **(A)** $120 \mathrm{~Hz}, 60 \mathrm{~Hz}$
- **(B)** $60 \mathrm{~Hz}, 120 \mathrm{~Hz}$
- **(C)** $60 \mathrm{~Hz}, 60 \mathrm{~Hz}$
- **(D)** $120 \mathrm{~Hz}, 120 \mathrm{~Hz}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $60 \mathrm{~Hz}, 120 \mathrm{~Hz}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

In a half-wave rectifier, one pulse is obtained for each wave and hence 60 pulses will be obtained per second. In a full wave rectifier, two pulses are obtained for each wave and 120 pulses are obtained per second.

### Q10. MHT CET 2007

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

When intensity of incident light increases

**Options:**

- **(A)** photo-current increases
- **(B)** photo-current decreases
- **(C)** kinetic energy of emitted photoelectrons increases
- **(D)** kinetic energy of emitted photoelectrons decreases

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) photo-current increases**

**Detailed Solution:**

According to Einstein's theory of photoelectric effect a single incident photon ejects a single electron. Therefore, when intensity increases, the number of incident photons increases, so number of ejected electrons increases, hence, photocurrent increases.
Now, maximum energy of $=\frac{1}{2} m v_{\max }^{2}$
and $\frac{1}{2} m v_{\max }^{2}=e V_{0}$, where $V_{0}$ is stopping potential.
Thus, the maximum kinetic energy of the
electrons does not depend upon the intensity
of the incident rays, because the stopping
potential is not affected by the increase of the
intensity of rays.

</details>
