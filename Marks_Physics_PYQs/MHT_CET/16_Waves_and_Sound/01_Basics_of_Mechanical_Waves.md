A body sends waves $150$ mm long through medium P and $0.30$ m long in medium Q. If the velocity of wave in medium P is $80$ cm/s, the velocity of wave in medium Q in m/s is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $1.6$
- **(B)** $2.0$
- **(C)** $3.2$
- **(D)** $3.8$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $1.6$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Given:

$f = \dfrac{v_P}{\lambda_P} = \dfrac{v_Q}{\lambda_Q}$

$v_Q = v_P \times \dfrac{\lambda_Q}{\lambda_P}$

$v_Q = 0.8 \times \dfrac{0.30}{0.15}$

$v_Q = 0.8 \times 2 = 1.6 \text{ m/s}$

### Q2. MHT CET 2026 (15 May Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

In a sine wave, minimum distance between $2$ particles always having same speed is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$
- **(B)** $\dfrac{\lambda}{4}$
- **(C)** $\dfrac{\lambda}{3}$
- **(D)** $\lambda$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Let the equation of a traveling sine wave be:

$y = A \sin(kx - \omega t)$

The velocity of a particle at position $x$ is given by:

$v = \dfrac{\partial y}{\partial t} = -A\omega \cos(kx - \omega t)$

The speed of the particle is the magnitude of its velocity:

$|v| = A\omega |\cos(kx - \omega t)|$

For two particles at positions $x_1$ and $x_2$ to always have the same speed, their speeds must be equal for all times $t$:

$|\cos(kx_1 - \omega t)| = |\cos(kx_2 - \omega t)|$

Squaring both sides to remove the absolute value, we get:

$\cos^2(kx_1 - \omega t) = \cos^2(kx_2 - \omega t)$

Using the identity $\cos^2 \theta = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$:

$\cos(2kx_1 - 2\omega t) = \cos(2kx_2 - 2\omega t)$

This implies:

$2kx_1 - 2\omega t = 2n\pi \pm (2kx_2 - 2\omega t)$

For this equation to hold true for all values of $t$, the time-dependent terms must cancel out. This is only possible if we take the positive sign:

$2kx_1 - 2\omega t = 2n\pi + 2kx_2 - 2\omega t$

$2k(x_1 - x_2) = 2n\pi$

$k \Delta x = n\pi$

Substituting the wave number $k = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}$:

$\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda} \Delta x = n\pi \Rightarrow \Delta x = \dfrac{n\lambda}{2}$

For the minimum non-zero distance between the two particles, we take $n = 1$:

$\Delta x = \dfrac{\lambda}{2}$

Answer: $\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$

</details>

**Options:**

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $0.225\ \text{N}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Comparing the given wave equation $Y = 0.05 \sin(x + 15t)$ with the standard wave equation $Y = A \sin(kx + \omega t)$, we get:

$k = 1\ \text{rad/m}$

$\omega = 15\ \text{rad/s}$

$v = \dfrac{\omega}{k} = \dfrac{15}{1} = 15\ \text{m/s}$

Substituting the given values $\mu = 10^{-3}\ \text{kg/m}$ and $v = 15\ \text{m/s}$:

$T = 10^{-3} \times (15)^2$

$T = 10^{-3} \times 225 = 0.225\ \text{N}$

### Q4. MHT CET 2026 (15 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

The speed of a wave on a string is $150$ m/s when the tension is $120$ N. The percentage increase in the tension to raise the wave speed by $20\%$ is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $44\%$
- **(B)** $40\%$
- **(C)** $20\%$
- **(D)** $10\%$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $44\%$**

**Detailed Solution:**

The speed of a transverse wave on a string is given by $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}}$

where $T$ is the tension and $\mu$ is the linear mass density.

From the formula, $v \propto \sqrt{T}$, which implies $T \propto v^2$.

Let the initial speed be $v_1$ and the initial tension be $T_1$.

The new speed is increased by $20\%$, so $v_2 = v_1 + 0.20v_1 = 1.2v_1$.

The new tension $T_2$ is given by:

$\dfrac{T_2}{T_1} = \left(\dfrac{v_2}{v_1}\right)^2 = (1.2)^2 = 1.44$

The percentage increase in tension is:

$\dfrac{T_2 - T_1}{T_1} \times 100\% = (1.44 - 1) \times 100\% = 44\%$

Answer: $44\%$

</details>

### Q14. MHT CET 2026 (11 April Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

A string of length $0.5$ m and mass $10^{-3}$ kg is tightly clamped at its ends. The tension in the string is $0.8$ N. Identical wave pulses are produced at one end at equal intervals of time $\Delta t$. What is the minimum value of $\Delta t$ which allows constructive interference between successive pulses?

**Options:**

- **(A)** $0.40$ s
- **(B)** $0.20$ s
- **(C)** $0.10$ s
- **(D)** $0.05$ s

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(D) $0.05$ s**

**Detailed Solution:**

The linear mass density of the string is given by:

$\mu = \dfrac{m}{L} = \dfrac{10^{-3}}{0.5} = 2 \times 10^{-3} \text{ kg/m}$

The speed of the transverse wave pulse on the string is:

$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{T}{\mu}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{0.8}{2 \times 10^{-3}}} = \sqrt{400} = 20 \text{ m/s}$

When a wave pulse is produced at one end, it travels to the other end, reflects, and comes back. Since the string is tightly clamped at both ends, the pulse undergoes a phase change of $\pi$ (inversion) upon reflection at the far end, and another phase change of $\pi$ upon reflection at the near end. 

After two reflections (one complete round trip), the total phase change is $2\pi$. This means the returning pulse is upright and traveling in the original direction, exactly matching its initial state.

For constructive interference to occur between successive pulses, the second pulse must be produced exactly when the first pulse has completed one round trip and is starting its second trip. 

The minimum time interval $\Delta t$ is the time taken for one round trip:

$\Delta t = \dfrac{2L}{v} = \dfrac{2 \times 0.5}{20} = \dfrac{1}{20} = 0.05 \text{ s}$

Answer: $0.05$ s

</details>

### Q59. MHT CET 2008

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

A wave travelling along a string is described by the equation $y=A \sin (\omega t-k x)$
The maximum particle velocity is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $A \omega$
- **(B)** $\omega / k$
- **(C)** $d \omega / d k$
- **(D)** $x / l$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $A \omega$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Given that, the displacement of a particle is
$$
y=A \sin (\omega t-k x) ...(i)
$$ 
The particle velocity
$$
v_{p}=\frac{d y}{d t} ...(ii)
$$
Now, on differentiating Eq. (i) w.r.t, $t$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{d y}{d t}=A \cos (\omega t-k x) \cdot \omega \\
\Rightarrow \quad & \frac{d y}{d t}=A \omega \cos (\omega t-k x)
\end{aligned}
$$
From Eq. (ii)
$$
\Rightarrow \quad v_{p}=A \omega \cos (\omega t-k x)
$$
For maximum particle velocity,
$$
\cos (\omega t-k x)=1
$$
So, $\quad v_{p}=A \omega \times 1$
$\Rightarrow$
$$
v_{p}=A \omega
$$

</details>

### Q60. MHT CET 2007

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

In a diode, when there is a saturation current, the plate resistance will be

**Options:**

- **(A)** data insufficient
- **(B)** zero
- **(C)** some finite quantity
- **(D)** infinite quantity

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(D) infinite quantity**

**Detailed Solution:**

Key Idea Change in current at saturation is
zero.
We know that plate resistance is given by
$$
r_{p}=\frac{\delta V}{\delta I}
$$
where $\delta V$ is change in voltage and $\delta I$ is change in current. Also, at saturation change in current is zero.
$\therefore$
$$
r_{p}=\frac{\delta V}{0}=\infty
$$
Hence, plate resistance will be infinite.

</details>
