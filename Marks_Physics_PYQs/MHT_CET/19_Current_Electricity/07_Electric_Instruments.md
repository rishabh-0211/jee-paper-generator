### Q1. MHT CET 2026 (20 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

A voltmeter with series resistance $R$ has a range $(0-V_1)$ volt. With a series resistance of $4R$, its range becomes $(0-V_2)$ volt. The relation between $V_1$ and $V_2$ is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $V_2 = 4V_1$
- **(B)** $V_2 < 4V_1$
- **(C)** $V_2 > 4V_1$
- **(D)** $V_2 = \dfrac{V_1}{4}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $V_2 < 4V_1$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Let $G$ be the resistance of the galvanometer and $I_g$ be the current for full scale deflection.

For the first case, the range of the voltmeter is $V_1$:
$V_1 = I_g(G + R)$

For the second case, the series resistance is $4R$ and the range is $V_2$:
$V_2 = I_g(G + 4R)$

Multiplying the first equation by $4$ gives:
$4V_1 = 4I_g(G + R) = 4I_g G + 4I_g R$

Subtracting the expression for $V_2$ from $4V_1$:
$4V_1 - V_2 = (4I_g G + 4I_g R) - (I_g G + 4I_g R)$

$4V_1 - V_2 = 3I_g G$

Since the galvanometer resistance $G$ and the full scale deflection current $I_g$ are both positive quantities, $3I_g G > 0$.

Therefore, $4V_1 - V_2 > 0 \Rightarrow V_2 < 4V_1$.

Answer: $V_2 < 4V_1$

</details>

### Q2. MHT CET 2026 (19 May Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

In a potentiometer circuit when two cells of emf $1.6$ V and $1.3$ V are connected to assist each other, the balancing length is $290$ cm. What will be the balancing length in cm when these two cells are connected in opposition?

**Options:**

- **(A)** $70$
- **(B)** $80$
- **(C)** $65$
- **(D)** $30$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(D) $30$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Let the potential gradient of the potentiometer wire be $k$.

When the cells assist each other, the equivalent emf is $E_1 + E_2$.

$E_1 + E_2 = k l_1$

$1.6 + 1.3 = k \times 290$

$2.9 = k \times 290$

When the cells oppose each other, the equivalent emf is $E_1 - E_2$.

$E_1 - E_2 = k l_2$

$1.6 - 1.3 = k l_2$

$0.3 = k l_2$

Dividing the two equations:

$\dfrac{2.9}{0.3} = \dfrac{290}{l_2}$

$l_2 = \dfrac{0.3 \times 290}{2.9} = 30$ cm

Answer: $30$

</details>

### Q7. MHT CET 2026 (14 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

A potentiometer wire has length $L$. For a given cell of e.m.f. $E$, the balancing length is $\dfrac{L}{3}$ from the positive end of the wire. If the length of potentiometer wire is increased by $50\%$ then for the same cell the balance point is obtained at length (from positive end)

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\dfrac{L}{2}$
- **(B)** $\dfrac{L}{5}$
- **(C)** $\dfrac{L}{3}$
- **(D)** $\dfrac{L}{4}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $\dfrac{L}{2}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Let the potential difference across the potentiometer wire be $V$.

Initial potential gradient of the wire is $k_1 = \dfrac{V}{L}$.

The balancing length for the cell of e.m.f. $E$ is $l_1 = \dfrac{L}{3}$.

Therefore, the e.m.f. of the cell is:
$E = k_1 l_1 = \left(\dfrac{V}{L}\right) \left(\dfrac{L}{3}\right) = \dfrac{V}{3}$

When the length of the potentiometer wire is increased by $50\%$, the new length becomes:
$L' = L + \dfrac{50}{100}L = \dfrac{3L}{2}$

Assuming the potential difference $V$ across the wire remains constant, the new potential gradient is:
$k_2 = \dfrac{V}{L'} = \dfrac{V}{\dfrac{3L}{2}} = \dfrac{2V}{3L}$

Let the new balancing length for the same cell be $l_2$. Then:
$E = k_2 l_2$

Substituting the values of $E$ and $k_2$:
$\dfrac{V}{3} = \left(\dfrac{2V}{3L}\right) l_2$

$l_2 = \dfrac{V}{3} \times \dfrac{3L}{2V} = \dfrac{L}{2}$

Answer: $\dfrac{L}{2}$

</details>

- **(D)** $Rn$

Resistance of the ammeter = $R$

### Q9. MHT CET 2026 (13 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

In a potentiometer, the null point is obtained on $7^{th}$ wire. What should we do to change the null point on $9^{th}$ wire by keeping all other quantities same ?

**Options:**

- **(A)** Decrease resistance in series with battery
- **(B)** Increase resistance in main Circuit
- **(C)** Decrease resistance in main circuit
- **(D)** Increase applied e.m.f.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) Increase resistance in main Circuit**

**Detailed Solution:**

The principle of a potentiometer is $E = kl$, where $E$ is the emf of the cell, $k$ is the potential gradient, and $l$ is the balancing length.

Since the null point shifts from the $7^{th}$ wire to the $9^{th}$ wire, the balancing length $l$ increases.

As the emf $E$ remains constant, an increase in $l$ means the potential gradient $k$ must decrease.

The potential gradient is given by $k = \dfrac{V}{L} = \dfrac{I R_w}{L}$, where $I$ is the current in the main circuit, $R_w$ is the resistance of the potentiometer wire, and $L$ is its total length.

To decrease $k$, the current $I$ in the main circuit must be decreased.

The current in the main circuit is $I = \dfrac{E_p}{R + R_w + r}$, where $R$ is the external resistance in the main circuit.

Therefore, to decrease $I$, the resistance $R$ in the main circuit must be increased.

When current $I$ is passed through galvanometer coil, it is deflected through $16^\circ$. But when it is shunted by a resistance of $8\,\Omega$, the same current produces a deflection of $4^\circ$. The resistance of the galvanometer is

- **(B)** $16\,\Omega$

- **(D)** $12\,\Omega$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $24\,\Omega$**

The deflection in a galvanometer is directly proportional to the current passing through it, $\theta \propto I_g$.

$I \propto 16^\circ$

$I_g' \propto 4^\circ$

Taking the ratio of the two currents:
$\dfrac{I_g'}{I} = \dfrac{4^\circ}{16^\circ} = \dfrac{1}{4}$

In a shunted galvanometer, the current through the galvanometer is given by:
$I_g' = I \left( \dfrac{S}{S + G} \right)$

Substituting the ratio:
$\dfrac{S}{S + G} = \dfrac{1}{4}$

$4S = S + G$

$G = 3S$

Given $S = 8\,\Omega$:
$G = 3 \times 8 = 24\,\Omega$

Answer: $24\,\Omega$

$E_1 = k l_1$

$E_1 - E_2 = k l_2$

Subtracting the second equation from the first equation:
$E_1 - (E_1 - E_2) = k l_1 - k l_2$
$E_2 = k(l_1 - l_2)$

Taking the ratio of $E_1$ and $E_2$:
$\dfrac{E_1}{E_2} = \dfrac{k l_1}{k(l_1 - l_2)} = \dfrac{l_1}{l_1 - l_2}$

The ratio $E_1 : E_2$ is $l_1 : (l_1 - l_2)$.

- **(C)** $900 \Omega$

### Q142. MHT CET 2008

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

A cell can be balanced against $110 \mathrm{~cm}$ and $100 \mathrm{~cm}$ of potentiometer wire, respectively with and without being short circuited through a resistance of $10 \Omega$. Its internal resistance is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $1.0 \Omega$
- **(B)** $0.5 \Omega$
- **(C)** $2.0 \Omega$
- **(D)** zero

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $1.0 \Omega$**

**Detailed Solution:**

In potentiometer experiment in which we find internal resistance of a cell, let $E$ be the emf of the cell and $V$ the terminal potential difference, then
$$
\frac{E}{V}=\frac{\mathrm{h}}{l_{2}}
$$
where $\mathrm{l}$ and $l_{2}$ are lengths of potentiometer wire with and without short circuited through a resistance.
Since, $\frac{E}{V}=\frac{R+r}{R}$
$\quad[\because E=I(R+r)$ and $V=I R]$
$\therefore \quad \frac{R+r}{R}=\frac{4}{l_{2}}$
or $\quad 1+\frac{r}{R}=\frac{110}{100}$
Or $\frac{r}{R}=\frac{10}{100}$
or $r=\frac{1}{10} \times 10=1 \Omega$

</details>

The resistance of an ammeter is $13 \Omega$ and its scale is graduated for a current upto $100 \mathrm{~A}$. After an additional shunt has been connected to this ammeter it becomes possible to measure currents upto $750 \mathrm{~A}$ by this meter. The value of shunt resistance is

- **(B)** $2 \Omega$
- **(C)** $0.2 \Omega$
- **(D)** $2 \mathrm{k} \Omega$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $2 \Omega$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Let $i_{a}$ is the current flowing through ammeter and $i$ is the total current. So, a current $i-i_{a}$ will flow through shunt resistance.

Potential difference across ammeter and shunt resistance is same.

ie, $\quad i_{a} \times R=\left(i-i_{a}\right) \times S$
