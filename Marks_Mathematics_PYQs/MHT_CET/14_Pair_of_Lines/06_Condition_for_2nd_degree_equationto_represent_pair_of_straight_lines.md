### Q1. MHT CET 2026 (20 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If the equation $ax^2 - y^2 + 2y + c = 1$ represents a pair of perpendicular lines, then the values of $a$ and $c$ respectively are...

**Options:**

- **(A)** $1, -1$
- **(B)** $0, 1$
- **(C)** $1, 0$
- **(D)** $2, 3$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $1, 0$**

**Detailed Solution:**

The given equation can be rewritten as $ax^2 - y^2 + 2y + c - 1 = 0$.

Comparing this with the general second-degree equation $Ax^2 + 2Hxy + By^2 + 2Gx + 2Fy + C = 0$, we get:
$A = a$, $H = 0$, $B = -1$, $G = 0$, $F = 1$, $C = c - 1$.

For the equation to represent a pair of perpendicular lines, the sum of the coefficients of $x^2$ and $y^2$ must be zero:
$A + B = 0$
$a - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow a = 1$

For the equation to represent a pair of straight lines, the condition $\Delta = 0$ must be satisfied:
$ABC + 2FGH - AF^2 - BG^2 - CH^2 = 0$

Substituting the values into the condition:
$(1)(-1)(c - 1) + 2(1)(0)(0) - (1)(1)^2 - (-1)(0)^2 - (c - 1)(0)^2 = 0$
$-(c - 1) - 1 = 0$
$-c + 1 - 1 = 0$
$c = 0$

Thus, the values of $a$ and $c$ are $1$ and $0$ respectively.

Answer: $1, 0$

</details>

$a = m$, $h = 0$, $b = -1$, $g = 0$, $f = 1$, $c = n - 3$.

### Q3. MHT CET 2026 (13 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If the equation $x^2 - y^2 - x + \lambda y - 2 = 0$ (where $\lambda > 0$) represents a pair of lines, then the coordinates of their point of intersection are:

**Options:**

- **(A)** $(1, 3)$
- **(B)** $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{3}{2}\right)$
- **(C)** $\left(\dfrac{3}{2}, \dfrac{1}{2}\right)$
- **(D)** $(0, -1)$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{3}{2}\right)$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Comparing the given equation $x^2 - y^2 - x + \lambda y - 2 = 0$ with the general equation of second degree $ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$, we get:

$a = 1$, $h = 0$, $b = -1$, $g = -\dfrac{1}{2}$, $f = \dfrac{\lambda}{2}$, $c = -2$

For the equation to represent a pair of straight lines, the condition is:

$abc + 2fgh - af^2 - bg^2 - ch^2 = 0$

Substituting the values:

$(1)(-1)(-2) + 0 - (1)\left(\dfrac{\lambda}{2}\right)^2 - (-1)\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2 - 0 = 0$

$2 - \dfrac{\lambda^2}{4} + \dfrac{1}{4} = 0$

$\dfrac{\lambda^2}{4} = \dfrac{9}{4} \Rightarrow \lambda^2 = 9$

Since $\lambda > 0$, we have $\lambda = 3$.

The equation of the pair of lines becomes:

$x^2 - y^2 - x + 3y - 2 = 0$

To find the point of intersection, we partially differentiate the equation with respect to $x$ and $y$ and equate them to zero:

$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2 - y^2 - x + 3y - 2) = 0 \Rightarrow 2x - 1 = 0 \Rightarrow x = \dfrac{1}{2}$

$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(x^2 - y^2 - x + 3y - 2) = 0 \Rightarrow -2y + 3 = 0 \Rightarrow y = \dfrac{3}{2}$

The coordinates of the point of intersection are $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{3}{2}\right)$.

Answer: $\left(\dfrac{1}{2}, \dfrac{3}{2}\right)$

</details>

### Q4. MHT CET 2026 (11 April Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If the equation $x^2 - ky^2 - 4x + 6y - 5 = 0$ represents a pair of straight lines, then their point of intersection is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $(1, 2)$
- **(B)** $(2, 3)$
- **(C)** $(3, 4)$
- **(D)** $(4, 5)$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) $(2, 3)$**

**Detailed Solution:**

The given equation is $x^2 - ky^2 - 4x + 6y - 5 = 0$.

Comparing this with the general second-degree equation $ax^2 + 2hxy + by^2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0$, we get:
$a = 1$, $h = 0$, $b = -k$, $g = -2$, $f = 3$, $c = -5$.

For the equation to represent a pair of straight lines, the condition is $abc + 2fgh - af^2 - bg^2 - ch^2 = 0$.

Substituting the values:
$(1)(-k)(-5) + 0 - (1)(3)^2 - (-k)(-2)^2 - 0 = 0$

$5k - 9 + 4k = 0$

$9k = 9 \Rightarrow k = 1$

So, the equation of the pair of straight lines is $x^2 - y^2 - 4x + 6y - 5 = 0$.

Let $F(x, y) = x^2 - y^2 - 4x + 6y - 5$.
The point of intersection of the lines is obtained by solving the partial derivatives $\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x} = 0$ and $\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y} = 0$.

$\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial x} = 2x - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 2$

$\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial y} = -2y + 6 = 0 \Rightarrow y = 3$

The point of intersection is $(2, 3)$.

Answer: $(2, 3)$

</details>

### Q5. MHT CET 2024 (09 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If an equation $\mathrm{hxy}+\mathrm{g} x+\mathrm{f} y+\mathrm{c}=0$ represents a pair of lines, then

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\mathrm{fg}=\mathrm{ch}$
- **(B)** $\mathrm{gh}=\mathrm{cf}$
- **(C)** $\mathrm{fh}=\mathrm{cg}$
- **(D)** $\mathrm{hf}=-\mathrm{cg}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $\mathrm{fg}=\mathrm{ch}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Given equation is $hxy + gx + fy + c = 0$.

Multiplying the entire equation by $h$, we get:

$h^2xy + hgx + hfy + hc = 0$

Rearranging and factoring the terms:

$hx(hy + g) + f(hy + g) - fg + hc = 0$

$(hx + f)(hy + g) + hc - fg = 0$

For the given equation to represent a pair of straight lines, it must be factorizable into two linear factors. This is only possible if the constant term in the above expression is zero.

Therefore, $hc - fg = 0$

$\Rightarrow fg = ch$

Answer: $\mathrm{fg}=\mathrm{ch}$

</details>

### Q6. MHT CET 2024 (02 May Shift 2)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If the equation $7 x^2-14 x y+p y^2-12 x+q y-4=0$ represents a pair of parallel lines then the value of $\sqrt{p^2+q^2-p q}$ is

**Options:**

- **(A)** $\sqrt{119}$
- **(B)** $\sqrt{107}$
- **(C)** $\sqrt{109}$
- **(D)** $\sqrt{108}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $\sqrt{109}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

Comparing the given equation $7 x^2 - 14 x y + p y^2 - 12 x + q y - 4 = 0$ with the general equation of second degree $a x^2 + 2 h x y + b y^2 + 2 g x + 2 f y + c = 0$, we get:

$a = 7$, $h = -7$, $b = p$, $g = -6$, $f = \dfrac{q}{2}$, $c = -4$

For the equation to represent a pair of parallel lines, the second-degree terms must form a perfect square, which requires $h^2 = a b$.

$(-7)^2 = 7 \times p \Rightarrow 49 = 7 p \Rightarrow p = 7$

Substituting $p = 7$ into the equation, the second-degree terms become $7 x^2 - 14 x y + 7 y^2 = 7(x - y)^2$.

The equation can now be written as:

$7(x - y)^2 - 12 x + q y - 4 = 0$

For this to represent a pair of parallel lines, the linear part must be a scalar multiple of $(x - y)$. Let $-12 x + q y = k(x - y)$.

Comparing the coefficients of $x$ and $y$, we get:

$k = -12$ and $-k = q$

$\Rightarrow q = 12$

We need to find the value of $\sqrt{p^2 + q^2 - p q}$. Substituting $p = 7$ and $q = 12$:

$\sqrt{7^2 + 12^2 - (7)(12)} = \sqrt{49 + 144 - 84} = \sqrt{109}$

Answer: $\sqrt{109}$

</details>
