### Q1. MHT CET 2026 (19 May Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `2`

If $pK_b$ value for fluoride ion at $25^\circ$C is $10.83$, calculate the ionisation constant of hydrofluoric acid in water at this temperature.

**Options:**

- **(A)** $1.74 \times 10^{-5}$
- **(B)** $3.52 \times 10^{-3}$
- **(C)** $6.75 \times 10^{-4}$
- **(D)** $5.38 \times 10^{-2}$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(C) $6.75 \times 10^{-4}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

For a conjugate acid-base pair, the relationship between the ionization constant of the acid ($K_a$) and the base ($K_b$) is given by:

$pK_a + pK_b = pK_w$

At $25^{\circ}$C, the ionic product of water $pK_w = 14$.

Given $pK_b$ for fluoride ion ($F^{-}$) is $10.83$.

$pK_a = 14 - 10.83 = 3.17$

The ionization constant of hydrofluoric acid (HF) is:

$K_a = 10^{-pK_a} = 10^{-3.17}$

$K_a = 10^{-4} \times 10^{0.83}$

Since $\log(6.75) \approx 0.83$, we have $10^{0.83} \approx 6.75$.

$K_a = 6.75 \times 10^{-4}$

Answer: $6.75 \times 10^{-4}$

</details>

### Q7. MHT CET 2026 (13 May Shift 1)

A weak monobasic acid is $18$ % dissociated in $0.05$ M solution. Calculate the concentration of solution if it is $10$ % dissociated .

- **(A)** $0.162$ M.
- **(B)** $0.141$ M.
- **(C)** $0.184$ M.
- **(D)** $0.125$ M.

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $0.162$ M.**

**Detailed Solution:**

For a weak monobasic acid, the dissociation constant $K_a$ is given by Ostwald's dilution law:

$K_a = C \alpha^2$

Since the acid is the same, $K_a$ remains constant at a given temperature. Therefore, we can write:

$C_1 \alpha_1^2 = C_2 \alpha_2^2$

Given values are:
$C_1 = 0.05$ M
$\alpha_1 = 18\% = 0.18$
$\alpha_2 = 10\% = 0.10$

Substituting these values into the equation:

$0.05 \times (0.18)^2 = C_2 \times (0.10)^2$

$0.05 \times 0.0324 = C_2 \times 0.01$

$0.00162 = C_2 \times 0.01$

$C_2 = \dfrac{0.00162}{0.01} = 0.162$ M

Answer: $0.162$ M.

</details>

### Q20. MHT CET 2026 (13 April Shift 1)

*Type:* `singleCorrect` | *Level:* `1`

What is the relationship between the degree of dissociation of a weak base and its concentration?

**Options:**

- **(A)** The degree of dissociation of a weak base is directly proportional to square root of its concentration.
- **(B)** The degree of dissociation of a weak base is inversely proportional to square root of its concentration.
- **(C)** The degree of dissociation of a weak base doesn't depend on concentration.
- **(D)** The degree of dissociation of a weak base is directly proportional to its concentration

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(B) The degree of dissociation of a weak base is inversely proportional to square root of its concentration.**

**Detailed Solution:**

For a weak base $BOH$, the dissociation reaction is $BOH \rightleftharpoons B^+ + OH^-$.

Let the initial concentration be $C$ and the degree of dissociation be $\alpha$.

At equilibrium, the concentrations are $[BOH] = C(1-\alpha)$, $[B^+] = C\alpha$, and $[OH^-] = C\alpha$.

The base dissociation constant $K_b$ is given by $K_b = \dfrac{[B^+][OH^-]}{[BOH]} = \dfrac{C\alpha \cdot C\alpha}{C(1-\alpha)} = \dfrac{C\alpha^2}{1-\alpha}$.

For a weak base, $\alpha$ is very small compared to $1$, so $1-\alpha \approx 1$.

Thus, $K_b \approx C\alpha^2 \Rightarrow \alpha = \sqrt{\dfrac{K_b}{C}}$.

Therefore, the degree of dissociation of a weak base is inversely proportional to the square root of its concentration.

Answer: The degree of dissociation of a weak base is inversely proportional to square root of its concentration.

</details>

### Q21. MHT CET 2025 (5 May Shift 2)

What is degree of dissociation of $\mathrm{CH}_3 \mathrm{COOH}$ if $\wedge^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{CH}_3 \mathrm{COO}^{-}\right)=50 \mathrm{~S} \mathrm{~cm}^2 \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$, $\wedge^{\circ}\left(\mathrm{H}^{+}\right)=350 \mathrm{~S} \mathrm{~cm}^2 \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ and molar conductivity of $5 \times 10^{-2} \mathrm{M} \mathrm{CH}_3 \mathrm{COOH}$ is $20 \mathrm{~S} \mathrm{~cm}^2 \mathrm{~mol}^{-1}$ ?

- **(B)** $\mathrm{NH}_4 \mathrm{Cl}$
- **(C)** $\mathrm{NH}_4 \mathrm{NO}_3$
- **(D)** $\mathrm{NaNO}_3$

<details>
<summary><b>View Answer & Solution</b></summary>

**Correct Option:** **(A) $\mathrm{NH}_4 \mathrm{CN}$**

**Detailed Solution:**

A solution that turns red litmus blue must be basic in nature.

$\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{CN}$ is a salt of a weak acid ($\mathrm{HCN}$) and a weak base ($\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{OH}$).

The dissociation constant of $\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{OH}$ ($K_b \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$) is greater than the dissociation constant of $\mathrm{HCN}$ ($K_a \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-10}$).

Since $K_b > K_a$, the anionic hydrolysis of $\mathrm{CN}^-$ is more extensive than the cationic hydrolysis of $\mathrm{NH}_4^+$, resulting in an excess of $\mathrm{OH}^-$ ions.

Thus, the aqueous solution of $\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{CN}$ is basic and turns red litmus blue.

$\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{Cl}$ and $\mathrm{NH}_4\mathrm{NO}_3$ are salts of a strong acid and a weak base, so their solutions are acidic.

$\mathrm{NaNO}_3$ is a salt of a strong acid and a strong base, so its solution is neutral.

Answer: $\mathrm{NH}_4 \mathrm{CN}$

</details>
